More notes on money, medicine and the past

Money, law and sport

On September 15, 2008, Lehman Brothers filed for the largest bankruptcy in American history, listing about 639 billion dollars in assets. The Federal Reserve and the Treasury had arranged a rescue for Bear Stearns that March, but they let Lehman fail after no buyer would take it without a government guarantee. Within days, money market funds that held its debt began to lose value, and banks around the world stopped lending to one another. Congress passed the 700 billion dollar Troubled Asset Relief Program on October 3, and Ben Bernanke later said that he had believed the Fed lacked the legal power to save the firm. Economists still debate whether a rescue would have contained the panic or only delayed it.

The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation took effect on May 25, 2018, and applies to any organization that handles the personal data of people in the EU, wherever the organization is based. It gives people the right to see the data held about them, to correct it and in many cases to have it erased. Regulators can fine a company up to 20 million euros or 4 percent of its worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. In May 2023, Ireland's Data Protection Commission fined Meta 1.2 billion euros for moving the data of European Facebook users to the United States. Critics argue that the rules have mainly produced consent pop-ups that most people click through without reading.

On October 12, 2019, in Vienna, Eliud Kipchoge of Kenya became the first person to run a marathon in under two hours, finishing in 1:59:40. The run does not count as a world record, because teams of pacemakers rotated in front of him and a car projected a laser line on the road to show the target pace. Kipchoge had narrowly missed the same goal at Monza in Italy in 2017, running 2:00:25. The official record now belongs to Kelvin Kiptum, also of Kenya, who ran 2:00:35 at the Chicago Marathon in October 2023, four months before he died in a car crash. Sports scientists still disagree about how much of Kipchoge's margin came from the pacing and how much from new carbon-plated shoes.

Medicine and nature

In September 1928, Alexander Fleming returned from a holiday to his laboratory at St Mary's Hospital in London and noticed that a mould growing on one of his culture plates had killed the bacteria around it. He named the active substance penicillin, but he could not purify it in useful amounts and turned to other work. Historians think the spores may have drifted up from a mycology laboratory on the floor below, although no one can be sure. A decade later, Howard Florey and Ernst Chain at the University of Oxford worked out how to extract and concentrate the drug, and in 1941 they treated their first patient, a policeman who improved for several days before the supply ran out. American factories then scaled up production so far that by D-Day in 1944 there was enough penicillin to treat Allied casualties.

Smallpox killed an estimated 300 million people in the 20th century alone before a global campaign wiped it out. In 1967 the World Health Organization launched an intensified eradication programme, led by the American epidemiologist Donald Henderson. Instead of trying to vaccinate everyone, teams found each new case and vaccinated the people around it, a strategy called ring vaccination that worked because the virus had no animal host to hide in. The last natural case was Ali Maow Maalin, a hospital cook in Merca, Somalia, who fell ill in October 1977 and recovered. The WHO declared smallpox eradicated on May 8, 1980, and it remains the only human disease ever eradicated.

In 1995 and 1996, the US Fish and Wildlife Service released 31 gray wolves from Canada into Yellowstone National Park, about 70 years after the last wolves there had been killed. The number of elk in the park's northern range fell from about 19,000 in 1994 to fewer than 5,000 by 2012. Some ecologists argue that the wolves set off a trophic cascade: elk browsed less along streams, so willows and aspens grew back and beavers returned. Others point out that hunting, drought and a rising number of bears also cut the elk herd, and that willows recovered in some places but not in others. The debate matters because the Yellowstone wolves are often cited as proof that restoring a single predator can repair a whole ecosystem.

Ice and the deep past

Thwaites Glacier in West Antarctica, sometimes called the Doomsday Glacier, holds enough ice to raise global sea level by about 65 centimeters. It is losing ice faster than it did in the 1990s, partly because warm ocean water reaches the underside of its floating ice shelf and melts it from below. Since 2018 the International Thwaites Glacier Collaboration, run by the US National Science Foundation and the UK Natural Environment Research Council, has sent teams to drill through the ice and lower a robot called Icefin beneath it. Their measurements suggest that the glacier could retreat faster later this century, but models disagree on whether that would take decades or centuries. Because its loss could also destabilize neighboring glaciers, some researchers think it matters more than its own 65 centimeters suggest.

Göbekli Tepe, a hilltop site in southeastern Turkey, holds circles of carved stone pillars that were raised around 9500 BC, thousands of years before Stonehenge. The T-shaped pillars, some more than five meters tall, are carved with foxes, boars, snakes and vultures. Klaus Schmidt of the German Archaeological Institute, who led excavations from 1995 until his death in 2014, argued that hunter-gatherers built it as a sanctuary and that the effort of building it may have pushed people toward farming. Later digs found houses, water cisterns and tools for grinding grain, so many archaeologists now think that people also lived there. Only a small part of the site has been excavated.

Jikji, a collection of Buddhist teachings printed at Heungdeok Temple in Cheongju in 1377, is the oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type. It predates the Gutenberg Bible, printed in Mainz around 1455, by about 78 years. Only the second of its two volumes survives, and it is held by the National Library of France in Paris, after a French diplomat took it from Korea in the late 19th century. Korea has asked for its return, but the library holds that it was acquired legally. UNESCO added it to the Memory of the World Register in 2001.

Art and proof

Ludwig van Beethoven's Ninth Symphony was first performed in Vienna on May 7, 1824, when the composer was almost completely deaf. It was the first symphony by a major composer to bring in singers, who in the last movement sing Friedrich Schiller's poem "Ode to Joy". According to several people who were there, a soloist had to turn Beethoven around at the end so that he could see the audience applauding, though their accounts differ in the details. The work lasts about 70 minutes, and a later legend holds that the compact disc was sized to fit it. In 1972 the Council of Europe adopted the melody of the final movement as its anthem, and the European Union took it up in 1985.

In February 2020, Parasite, directed by Bong Joon-ho, became the first film not in English to win the Academy Award for Best Picture. It had already won the Palme d'Or at the Cannes Film Festival the previous May, the first Korean film to do so. The story follows the poor Kim family as they talk their way, one by one, into jobs in the house of the wealthy Park family in Seoul. Accepting a Golden Globe a month earlier, Bong told the audience: "Once you overcome the one-inch-tall barrier of subtitles, you will be introduced to so many more amazing films." The film earned more than 250 million dollars worldwide, on a budget of about 11 million dollars.

Around 1637, Pierre de Fermat wrote in the margin of his copy of Diophantus's Arithmetica that no positive whole numbers a, b and c satisfy a^n + b^n = c^n for any n greater than 2, adding that he had "a truly marvellous proof of this, which this margin is too narrow to contain". No such proof was ever found among his papers, and most mathematicians doubt that he had one. In June 1993, Andrew Wiles announced a proof at a lecture in Cambridge after seven years of working largely in secret. A reviewer found a gap in it, and Wiles closed the gap the following year with his former student Richard Taylor. The proof was published in the Annals of Mathematics in 1995, and Wiles received the Abel Prize for it in 2016.
