The pier opened in the spring after two years of work on its pilings, and the fishing boats moved over first because the old berths were failing fast.

By the end of that summer the old pier stood empty, and the town began to argue about what to do with it, which took most of the decade.

Tearing the old pier down would cost more than the town had set aside for it, and leaving it up would cost more each year in the insurance alone.

The survey found that the southern third of the pier had kept its strength, while the northern end had lost most of the timber below the tide line.

It was not enough to settle the argument, because each side read the same figures as proof of what it had said all along about the town budget.

The walkway opened with a brass band and a good deal of rain, and within a month it had become the place where everyone went on an evening.

The tide tables on the kiosk wall were the first thing anyone asked about, so the harbor master began to post them by hand every single week.

The ferry timetable changed twice that decade, once for the new ramp and once when the mainland terminal moved to the far end of the bay.

Winter storms took the last of the northern pilings in a single night, which saved the town the cost of pulling them out by barge that spring.

The museum itself began as a shelf in the harbor office, with a brass lamp, a ledger and a jar of old rivets that nobody could bear to throw away.

School groups come through every spring and are allowed to hold the rivets, which is the part they remember and the part they talk about on the ferry.

Nobody knows for certain, though the ledger records a pair of boots drying on the harbor office stove in the same week the stories say the dog arrived.

Fishing never came back to what it had been before the channel silted, but the crews that stayed moved to crab and did better than they had feared.

That second ledger is the one the historians ask for, because it runs unbroken for forty years and gives the weather as well as the catch each day.

The band still plays on the walkway on the first dry Sunday of each summer, although it has been some years since anyone agreed on what to play.
