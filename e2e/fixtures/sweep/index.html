The harbor almanac

The pier opened in the spring after two years of work on its pilings, and the fishing boats moved over first because the old berths had silted up over the winter. The ferry followed them once the new ramp had passed its load tests, which took most of the month.

By the end of that summer the old pier stood empty, and the town began to argue about what to do with it. Most of the argument was about money, as these arguments usually are, and neither side had a figure that the other side would accept.

A short note, too short to fold.

Tearing the old pier down would cost more than the town had set aside for it, and leaving it up would cost less this year and far more once the timbers began to rot. The surveyors said that would take about a decade, give or take a hard winter.

What the survey found

The survey found that the southern third of the pier had kept its strength, while the northern end had lost a quarter of its load rating. That was enough to close the northern end to vehicles at once, and the fishing crews grumbled about the longer walk.

It was not enough to settle the argument, because each side read the same figures as proof of what it had said all along. A year later the town voted to keep the southern third as a walkway and to take down the rest over the following two summers.

The walkway opened with a brass band and a good deal of rain, and within a month it had become the place where people took their visitors. A kiosk sold coffee at one end, and the town put benches along the side that faced away from the wind.

The tide tables on the kiosk wall were the first thing anyone asked about, so the harbor master began to chalk the day's times on a board beside them. The board stayed long after the printed tables had faded, and people still photograph it.

This paragraph lives in a small pane that scrolls on its own, and it says what the logbook kept in that pane records, which is the depth of the channel at the turn of each tide through the whole of one year on the water.

This second paragraph in the pane says how the readings were taken, which was with a weighted line from the end of the pier at first and then with a small sounder that the school lent to the harbor office for the season.

This third paragraph in the pane starts out below the part that shows, and it has enough words in it to count as a paragraph that folds, so a sweep inside the pane can reach it after the pane has scrolled a little way down.

The ferry timetable changed twice that decade, once for the new ramp and once when the mainland terminal moved, and both times the crew printed the new hours by hand and pinned them to the gangway for a week before the printer caught up.

Winter storms took the last of the northern pilings in a single night, which saved the town the cost of pulling them out. Divers found most of them a mile down the coast the next spring, and two now stand as gateposts outside the museum.

The museum itself began as a shelf in the harbor office, with a brass lamp, a ledger and a jar of old rivets, and it grew whenever somebody cleared out a shed. It moved into the customs house when the customs service finally left the island.

School groups come through every spring and are allowed to hold the rivets, which is the part they remember. The curator keeps a tally of what they ask, and the most common question has not changed in thirty years: who was the first to fall in?

Nobody knows for certain, though the ledger records a pair of boots drying on the harbor office stove in the first week the pier was open. The boots belonged to the ferry's second mate, who always said that he had only stepped in to save a dog.

The dog appears nowhere in the ledger, which the second mate's family takes as proof of nothing at all. His granddaughter runs the kiosk now, and she will tell the story either way depending on how much coffee you buy from her that morning.

Fishing never came back to what it had been before the channel silted, but the crews that stayed moved to crab, and the crab did well. The harbor office began to keep its own record of the catch, in a second ledger with a green spine.

That second ledger is the one the historians ask for, because it runs unbroken for forty years and gives the water temperature beside the catch. Nobody planned this; the clerk who started it simply liked to know how cold his hands ought to be.

The clerk's habit turned out to matter a good deal when the university came looking for long records of the coast. They copied the ledger over three weeks in the customs house and paid for the new roof on the museum as their thanks.

A new roof meant that the upper floor could open, and the upper floor meant more room for what people kept bringing in. There are now four brass lamps, two figureheads and a whole wall of photographs that nobody has yet managed to date.

Some of the photographs can be dated from the state of the pier in the background, which has made the survey drawings unexpectedly useful. A retired engineer spends his Tuesdays matching pilings to pictures and has placed nearly two hundred so far.

He says that the work is mostly a matter of counting, and that the pictures are much better company than the surveyors were. He has asked that his own photograph, when the time comes, be filed under the year the pier opened and not the year he did.

The walkway needs new decking about every twenty years, and the last time it came due the town paid for it by selling a plank to anyone who would have their name cut into it. They sold out in nine days and ordered a second lot.

Visitors now walk over the names of most of the families on the island, along with a number of their dogs. The harbor master maintains that this is the only thing the town has ever agreed on, and nobody has yet written in to correct him.

The band still plays on the walkway on the first dry Sunday of each summer, although it has been some years since anyone could say which Sunday that would be in advance. The kiosk does good business in umbrellas on the Saturday before.

Tide and weather still decide most things here, and the island has learned to plan around them without much complaint. The ferry goes when it can, the crab boats go when they must, and the board beside the kiosk tells you which is which.

The second note in this column says that visitors may read the ledgers on request, on the upper floor of the customs house, so long as they leave their coffee downstairs and ask the curator before they turn any of the older pages.
